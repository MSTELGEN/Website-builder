## Part 1: Environment (8 pages)

**Chapter 1: Bedroom Temperature — The Full Protocol**
- Mechanism deep-dive: the full vasodilation-core temperature drop pathway and why 18°C is the research consensus number, not an arbitrary recommendation
- Seasonal adaptation: how to manage the protocol in summer without air conditioning
- Partner temperature conflict: evidence-based compromise strategies (dual-zone bedding, fan placement, mattress pads)
- The "thermoneutral zone" — the temperature band in which the body can thermoregulate without waking: 16°C–19°C for most adults; lower for women in the luteal phase
- What sleep trackers show when temperature is wrong vs. corrected
- *Citation: Walker, M. (2017). Why We Sleep. Czeisler, C.A. et al. (multiple). Sleep thermoregulation studies.*

**Chapter 2: Light Pollution and Blackout**
- Melanopsin and intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells (ipRGCs): the photoreceptors that detect light for the circadian clock rather than for vision — and why they're sensitive to even dim light
- The lux threshold: research shows that light above 10 lux in the hour before bed measurably suppresses melatonin; most bedside lamps emit 100–400 lux
- Blackout vs. eye mask: which is more effective and why (blackout is structurally superior; eye masks are a good second)
- Street light, alarm clocks, and device standby lights — the overlooked sources
- Practical: how to audit your bedroom light environment in 10 minutes
- Morning light: the circadian anchor — why 10–30 minutes of morning sunlight is as important as evening darkness
- *Citation: Czeisler, C.A. et al. (2001). Exposure to room light before bedtime suppresses melatonin onset. Journal of Clinical Endocrinology & Metabolism. Provencio, I. et al. (2000). Melanopsin: An opsin in human eye. Journal of Neuroscience.*

**Chapter 3: Sound Environment**
- The difference between constant sound and variable sound: white noise and pink noise work not because silence is impossible but because they mask the sharp transitions (a door closing, a car) that trigger micro-arousals
- The research on pink noise specifically: 2017 study by Papalambros et al. showing pink noise synchronised with slow oscillations increases slow-wave sleep and memory consolidation in older adults
- Decibel thresholds: sounds above 45dB during sleep measurably fragment architecture; most street noise in urban environments runs 50–70dB
- Practical options: white noise machine, fan, HEPA air filter (dual function), foam earplugs (NRR 33 = ~30dB reduction)
- Partner snoring: what the research says works (positional therapy for mild snoring; CPAP assessment for moderate-severe)
- *Citation: Papalambros, N.A. et al. (2017). Acoustic enhancement of sleep slow oscillations and concomitant memory improvement in older adults. Frontiers in Human Neuroscience.*

**Chapter 4: Mattress, Pillow, and Position**
- Mattress firmness and sleep quality: the 2015 Kovacs et al. RCT showing medium-firm mattresses reduced chronic low-back pain and improved sleep quality vs. firm — and why "firm is better for your back" is not supported by current evidence
- Pillow height and cervical alignment: the Gordon 2010 protocol for finding correct pillow height by sleep position (side vs. back vs. front)
- Sleep position evidence: Skarpsno 2017 data on position and non-restorative sleep; glymphatic clearance and lateral positioning (emerging research, note uncertainty)
- Left-side specifics: gastric emptying, acid reflux reduction, cardiac load in pregnancy
- The pillow-between-knees protocol: why it reduces hip flexor strain and lower back rotation during side sleeping
- *Citation: Gordon, S.J. et al. (2010). Pillow use and neck pain. Manual Therapy. Skarpsno, E.S. et al. (2017). Nature and Science of Sleep. Kovacs, F.M. et al. (2015). Spine.*

## Part 2: Body (8 pages)

**Chapter 5: Breathing Protocols — Three Variations**
- The physiology of vagal tone: why exhale-dominant breathing activates the parasympathetic nervous system and why this is the correct neurological state for sleep onset
- **Variation 1: The 4-6 Protocol** (beginner) — 4-count inhale, 6-count exhale; appropriate for anyone new to structured breathing; target: 5–6 cycles per minute
- **Variation 2: The 4-7-8 Protocol** (intermediate) — 4-count inhale, 7-count hold, 8-count exhale; developed from pranayama practice; evidence for anxiolytic effect from Zelano et al. 2016 (nasal breathing and hippocampal fear response)
- **Variation 3: Cyclic Sighing** (advanced) — double inhale through nose followed by extended exhale through mouth; 2023 Stanford study by Balban et al. showing superiority over mindfulness meditation for acute stress reduction; most efficient for pre-sleep cortisol reduction
- How to choose your variation; how to progress; how long before you see measurable HRV improvement
- *Citation: Jerath, R. et al. (2015). Applied Psychophysiology and Biofeedback. Zelano, C. et al. (2016). Nasal respiration entrains human limbic oscillations. Journal of Neuroscience. Balban, M.Y. et al. (2023). Brief structured respiration practices enhance mood and reduce physiological arousal. Cell Reports Medicine.*

**Chapter 6: Foot Bath Rituals — The Full Protocol**
- Expanded mechanism: arteriovenous anastomoses, peripheral vasodilation, and the exact physiological pathway from warm feet to core temperature drop
- The Haghayegh 2019 meta-analysis in full: 13 studies, 1,047 participants, 10-minute average reduction in sleep onset latency from correctly timed warm bathing
- Temperature precision: 38°C vs. 40°C vs. 43°C — what changes at each threshold and why above 42°C reverses the benefit
- Timing window: the 60–90 minute post-bath window and what happens if you go to bed immediately
- Foot bath protocol (step-by-step), shower protocol, and full bath protocol — which produces the largest effect and why
- Enhancements: Epsom salts (magnesium absorption through skin — evidence is modest but the ritual benefit is real), lavender (evidence: Koulivand et al. 2013, modest but genuine)
- *Citation: Haghayegh, S. et al. (2019). Sleep Medicine Reviews. Koulivand, P.H. et al. (2013). Lavender and the nervous system. Evidence-Based Complementary and Alternative Medicine.*

**Chapter 7: Magnesium — Forms, Timing, and Dosage**
- Why magnesium matters for sleep: GABA receptor agonism, NMDA receptor regulation, and cortisol pathway involvement — three separate mechanisms
- The six common forms and what the evidence says about each:
  - **Magnesium glycinate**: highest bioavailability, least GI disruption, best evidence for sleep — recommended
  - **Magnesium threonate**: crosses the blood-brain barrier, emerging evidence for cognitive effects; more expensive, less sleep-specific evidence than glycinate
  - **Magnesium oxide**: cheapest, poorest bioavailability (~4%), primarily used as a laxative — not recommended for sleep
  - **Magnesium citrate**: moderate bioavailability, GI effects at higher doses
  - **Magnesium malate**: good for daytime energy metabolism, less relevant for sleep
  - **Magnesium L-taurate**: emerging, limited evidence
- Dosage: 200–400mg elemental magnesium 30–60 minutes before bed (note: total daily intake from food + supplement should not exceed 400mg from supplements for most adults — UK NHS guidance)
- Contraindications: kidney disease, certain medications (antibiotics, diuretics) — full checklist
- Food sources: the dietary-first argument (100g pumpkin seeds = 550mg; dark chocolate, cashews, spinach)
- The "magnesium deficiency is widespread" claim: what the evidence actually shows (borderline low intakes are common in Western diets; clinical deficiency is rare in otherwise healthy adults)
- *Citation: Abbasi, B. et al. (2012). The effect of magnesium supplementation on primary insomnia. Journal of Research in Medical Sciences. Nielsen, F.H. & Lukaski, H.C. (2006). Update on the relationship between magnesium and exercise. Magnesium Research.*

**Chapter 8: Mouth Breathing — The Fix**
- The nasal vs. oral breathing distinction: nitric oxide production, filtration, humidity, and why nasal breathing produces measurably different physiological states than mouth breathing
- The sleep relevance: mouth breathing is associated with higher rates of snoring, sleep apnea, and lower sleep quality even in people without diagnosed apnea (Fitzpatrick et al. and Abreu et al.)
- Mouth taping protocol: how to use, which products, safety considerations, who should not use it (anyone with nasal obstruction, significant congestion, or respiratory conditions)
- Nasal breathing training: Buteyko technique fundamentals — the BOLT score as a baseline metric, carbon dioxide tolerance exercises
- When to investigate further: if you can't nasal-breathe through a normal conversation without distress, this is a structural or allergic issue requiring clinical assessment
- *Citation: Abreu, R.R. et al. (2008). Etiology, clinical manifestations and concurrent findings in mouth-breathing children. Journal de Pediatria. McKeown, P. (2015). The Oxygen Advantage.*

## Part 3: Mind (6 pages)

**Chapter 9: Paradoxical Intention**
- The counterintuitive core insight: trying to fall asleep activates performance anxiety, which activates cortisol and the sympathetic nervous system — the precise opposite of the neurological state required for sleep
- The Espie model: Colin Espie's work on psychophysiological insomnia and the "hyperarousal-monitoring" cycle — how watching yourself not sleep worsens the condition
- Paradoxical intention protocol: instructions to lie in bed and try to stay awake with eyes open; the mechanism by which this reduces performance pressure and allows sleep onset
- Evidence: Ascher & Efran (1978) original study; Broomfield & Espie (2003) RCT showing significant sleep onset reduction vs. control in chronic insomnia population
- How to combine paradoxical intention with stimulus control (bed = sleep only; get up if awake for 20+ minutes)
- Who it works best for: sleep-onset insomnia with performance anxiety component; less effective for sleep-maintenance insomnia
- *Citation: Espie, C.A. (2002). Insomnia: Conceptual issues in the development, persistence, and treatment. Annual Review of Psychology. Broomfield, N.M. & Espie, C.A. (2003). Initial insomnia and paradoxical intention. Behavioural and Cognitive Psychotherapy.*

**Chapter 10: The Last Hour Protocol**
- The 60-minute pre-sleep window as a "neurological runway" — what the brain needs to do in the final hour to set up correct sleep architecture
- What to remove: news, social media, emotionally activating content, work email, financial stress — each with the specific neural mechanism explaining why (amygdala activation, cortisol spike, dopamine loop)
- What to add: the evidence-supported options — reading fiction (stronger evidence than non-fiction for inducing sleep onset), light stretching, journaling (specifically the "tomorrow's to-do list" protocol from Scullin et al. 2018, which offloads task anxiety)
- The "worry window" technique: scheduling 15 minutes of deliberate worry earlier in the evening so it's less likely to intrude at bedtime
- Lighting protocol for the last hour: 10–40 lux target (candlelight equivalent; amber reading lamp)
- Temperature start (links back to Chapter 1): begin cooling the room in this window, not at lights-out
- *Citation: Scullin, M.K. et al. (2018). The effects of bedtime writing on difficulty falling asleep. Experimental Psychology.*

**Chapter 11: The Sunday Night Reset**
- Why Sunday night is statistically the worst sleep night of the week (social jet lag, Monday cortisol anticipation, changed weekend schedule)
- Social jet lag defined: Roenneberg et al. research showing that weekend sleep-time shifts of >1 hour create measurable circadian disruption equivalent to travelling one time zone
- The Sunday Reset protocol: fixed wake time (do not sleep in more than 45 minutes later than weekday wake time), evening light management, no alcohol, the full breathing protocol
- How to manage the transition from weekend to weekday without disrupting the circadian anchor
- The "circadian inertia" problem: why the first days of a corrected schedule feel worse before they feel better
- *Citation: Roenneberg, T. et al. (2012). Social jet lag and obesity. Current Biology.*

## Part 4: Food and Timing (8 pages)

**Chapter 12: The 3-Hour Dinner Rule — Expanded**
- Full mechanism: postprandial thermogenesis, insulin's effect on growth hormone suppression, and the cortisol rebound cycle from glycemic crashes
- Spiegel et al. research in full: the link between late eating, leptin suppression, and cortisol elevation — and why this specifically damages slow-wave sleep
- The glycemic index guide for evening eating: which foods are safe closer to bedtime and which cause the 2–4am cortisol rebound
- The tryptophan argument: foods that promote serotonin availability (walnuts, turkey, oats, eggs, dairy) and how to incorporate them into an earlier dinner without the late-eating penalty
- When the 3-hour rule is genuinely difficult (shift work, social dinners): mitigation strategies that reduce impact without full compliance
- *Citation: Spiegel, K. et al. (2004). Annals of Internal Medicine. Crispim, C.A. et al. (2011). Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine.*

**Chapter 13: Foods That Promote Sleep — The Research-Backed List**
- **Tart cherry juice**: 2010 Pigeon et al. RCT showing improved sleep efficiency in older adults; mechanism is melatonin content (naturally occurring, not supplemented); dose: 240ml twice daily in the study
- **Kiwifruit**: 2011 Lin et al. RCT (small, note sample size); serotonin content and possible antioxidant mechanism; effect size modest but replicated
- **Fatty fish (salmon, mackerel)**: omega-3 and vitamin D combination; Madore et al. 2020 showing DHA increases melatonin and serotonin brain levels in animal models; human RCT data limited
- **Walnuts**: melatonin content; tryptophan; reasonable pre-sleep snack if hunger strikes
- **Oats**: slow-digesting carbohydrate + melatonin precursors; appropriate as a light pre-sleep option if meal was 3+ hours prior
- **Milk (warm)**: tryptophan + psychological association with sleep — the psychological component is not trivial; conditioned response is real
- **Honest caveat**: no food will fix poor sleep architecture. These are modest additions, not interventions. Prioritise the structural rituals first.
- *Citation: Pigeon, W.R. et al. (2010). Effects of a tart cherry juice beverage on the sleep of older adults. Journal of Medicinal Food. Lin, H.H. et al. (2011). Effect of kiwifruit consumption on sleep quality. Asia Pacific Journal of Clinical Nutrition.*

**Chapter 14: Caffeine Math**
- The adenosine mechanism: caffeine works by blocking adenosine receptors, not by producing energy — the adenosine continues accumulating behind the block and releases when caffeine clears, causing the crash
- Half-life: caffeine's half-life in healthy adults is 5–7 hours. At 10 hours, 25% remains. At 14 hours, 12.5% remains.
- The personal cut-off calculation: [wake time] + [desired sleep time] − 10 hours = latest caffeine intake. Example: wake 7am, sleep 11pm, latest caffeine = 1pm.
- CYP1A2 gene variation: fast metabolisers (half-life ~3–4 hours) vs. slow metabolisers (half-life ~9–12 hours) — genetic variation explains why some people can drink coffee at 6pm and sleep fine; most people cannot
- Hidden caffeine sources: decaf coffee (~2–15mg per cup), green tea (~30–50mg), chocolate (20–60mg per 100g of dark chocolate), some medications
- Drake, C. et al. 2013 study: caffeine taken 6 hours before bed reduced sleep by 1 hour. This is the study most often cited wrong — the reduction is in sleep *time*, not just onset.
- *Citation: Drake, C. et al. (2013). Caffeine effects on sleep taken 0, 3, or 6 hours before going to bed. Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine.*

**Chapter 15: Alcohol Timing — The Full Picture**
- Full Ebrahim meta-analysis breakdown: what happened to each sleep stage at low, moderate, and high alcohol doses across multiple studies
- The REM suppression mechanism: alcohol metabolises into acetaldehyde, which suppresses REM-generating neurons in the brainstem; the more alcohol, the longer the suppression; the effect peaks 4–5 hours after drinking
- Why the "sleep aid" belief persists: sedation is real; sleep is partially real (NREM increases in first half); what's missing is the REM rebound disruption in the second half
- The dose-response relationship: even 0.5 units of alcohol 30 minutes before bed measurably alters sleep architecture in controlled studies
- The 3-hour rule revisited: why 3 hours is the minimum, not the optimum — for optimal REM architecture, 4+ hours is better
- Women and alcohol: women's liver enzyme activity (ADH and ALDH) is on average lower; women metabolise alcohol more slowly per unit of body weight than men; the 3-hour rule is more conservative for women
- *Citation: Ebrahim, I.O. et al. (2013). Alcoholism: Clinical and Experimental Research. Ebrahim et al. (2018). PLOS ONE.*

## Part 5: The 30-Night Protocol (4 pages)

**Chapter 16: How to Stack Rituals**
- The rationale for sequencing: some interventions interfere with accurate assessment of others if introduced simultaneously; some build on each other physiologically
- **Week 1 (Nights 1–7):** Environment (temperature + light + phone) — the fastest feedback loop; most people see measurable change here
- **Week 2 (Nights 8–14):** Timing (dinner + alcohol) — requires 3–5 nights for blood sugar and hormone normalisation; assess only from Night 5 of this block
- **Week 3 (Nights 15–21):** Body (breathing + foot bath + sleep position) — stack with environment and timing already in place
- **Week 4 (Nights 22–30):** Mind (paradoxical intention + last hour protocol + Sunday reset) — the highest-order interventions; they work best on a body that's already sleeping better
- What to track: a single daily rating (1–5 for sleep quality) plus the "did I do the ritual" yes/no; no need for a sleep tracker, though one adds useful data
- *Citation: Espie, C.A. et al. (2019). Digital cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia. The Lancet Psychiatry.*

**Chapter 17: Tracking Sheets**
- 30-night tracking grid (printable) — all rituals across 30 nights
- Weekly review template: what to assess at the end of each week block
- "What moved" and "what didn't" framework: how to interpret your own data without over-indexing on single nights

**Chapter 18: When to Seek Help**
- Red flags that indicate clinical assessment rather than self-help:
  - Loud snoring, gasping, or breath-holding witnessed by a partner → possible obstructive sleep apnea; requires polysomnography
  - Persistent insomnia despite 4+ weeks of structured sleep hygiene → refer for CBT-I (cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia), the gold-standard clinical treatment
  - Sleep disruption with perimenopausal symptoms (hot flushes, night sweats) → hormonal assessment; sleep hygiene alone insufficient
  - Restless legs / periodic limb movements → clinical assessment for iron deficiency, dopamine dysfunction
  - Excessive daytime sleepiness despite adequate sleep opportunity → possible narcolepsy, idiopathic hypersomnia; requires specialist review
- How to access CBT-I: Sleepio (digital CBT-I, NHS-endorsed in UK), licensed clinical psychologist, sleep clinic referral
- *Citation: Trauer, J.M. et al. (2015). Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Chronic Insomnia. Annals of Internal Medicine.*
